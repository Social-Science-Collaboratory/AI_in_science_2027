# Writing tips from *The Elements of Style*

Rule wording in quotes is William Strunk Jr.'s 1918 edition, which is in the public domain ([Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/37134)).

---

## Structure

- **"Make the paragraph the unit of composition: one paragraph to each topic."** If a paragraph covers both the method and its limits, split it in two.
- **"As a rule, begin each paragraph with a topic sentence, end it in conformity with the beginning."** A reader skimming only first sentences should still follow the argument. Don't end a paragraph on a side point or a minor detail.
- **"Express co-ordinate ideas in similar form."** Give parallel ideas parallel grammar: "We collected, cleaned, and analyzed the data," not "We collected the data, cleaning it, and then an analysis was run."
- **"In summaries, keep to one tense."** Abstracts and literature reviews often drift between past and present tense. Pick one.

## Sentences

- **"Use the active voice."** "We excluded 12 participants" says who did it. "Twelve participants were excluded" hides it. Strunk allows the passive when the thing acted on is the real topic of the paragraph, so "Responses were coded by two raters" is fine in a section about the responses.
- **"Put statements in positive form."** Write "the effect was small," not "the effect was not very large." "Did not support" is often just "contradicted," and "did not pay attention to" is "ignored."
- **"Use definite, specific, concrete language."** "Accuracy fell from 91% to 64%" beats "performance degraded substantially."
- **"Omit needless words."** "Due to the fact that" means "because." "It is important to note that" usually means nothing. Strunk singles out *the fact that* as a phrase to cut from every sentence it appears in. "The fact that the manipulation failed" is "the failed manipulation."
- **"Avoid a succession of loose sentences."** A string of "X, and Y" sentences reads like a list. Vary the structure so the logic shows.
- **"Keep related words together."** Keep the subject close to its verb, and keep a modifier next to the word it modifies. "We only found two effects" should be "We found only two effects."
- **"Place the emphatic words of a sentence at the end."** End a sentence on the finding, not on the citation or a hedge. The same goes for paragraphs: put the main point last.

## Punctuation and usage

- **"Form the possessive singular of nouns by adding 's."** This holds whatever the last letter is: "Cohen's *d*," "Jones's model," "the class's mean."
- **"In a series of three or more terms with a single conjunction, use a comma after each term except the last."** This is the serial comma: "accuracy, recall, and precision."
- **"Enclose parenthetic expressions between commas."** "The sample, however, was small." Use both commas, not just one.
- **"Place a comma before a conjunction introducing a co-ordinate clause."** "The model converged, but the estimates were unstable."
- **"Do not join independent clauses by a comma."** That's a comma splice. Use a period or a semicolon.
- **"Do not break sentences in two."** A fragment like "We ran a second study. To rule out demand effects." should be one sentence.
- **"A participial phrase at the beginning of a sentence must refer to the grammatical subject."** "Using a mixed model, the data showed…" says the data used the model. Write "Using a mixed model, we found…"

## Added in E. B. White's later editions

These are paraphrased, since that text is still under copyright.

- **Write with nouns and verbs.** Adjectives and adverbs rarely carry the claim.
- **Don't overstate, and don't over-qualify.** "Very," "rather," and "quite" weaken most sentences. One hedge per claim is enough.
