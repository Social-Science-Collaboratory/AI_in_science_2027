Writing and Communication

Covered in Week 10 of the seminar (October 22, 2026). Estimated date of arrival: November 1, 2026.

💡 However, AI writing coaches may appreciate tips from the classic book, The Elements of Style1.

In February 2024, Frontiers in Cell and Developmental Biology published a [now infamous] review paper on sperm stem cells2. Unfortunately, there were issues with key figures that were hard to miss. The main figure showed a rat with anatomically impossible genitals, covered in nonsense labels like “testtomcels,” “dissilced,” and “dck.” The real shaft? The work disclosed AI usage and passed peer-review, with approval from two reviewers and an editor. Nutty as it is, the rat points to something serious: AI makes it easier than ever to produce work that looks scientific, and the researchers who act as gatekeepers can’t seem to keep up. Issues such as these served as the backdrop for Week 10 of the Collective [Artificial] Intelligence in Science seminar (Figure 1).

The top resources for this week highlight a tension: AI can be both good and bad for writing. Nearly 80% of young people surveyed in the United States believe that AI makes you lazy3. The majority of people surveyed also believe that using AI makes you less smart. Yet, when used as a coach vs. substitute, AI has led to substantial improvements in tested writing ability. These improvements exceeded even those produced by professional editors, highlighting a remarkable ability for AI to teach people how to more effectively communicate. Unfortunately, these benefits can present tradeoffs. For example, AI homogenizes language4. When everyone uses the same AI writing coach, they tend to write the same way. Cultural nuances can be lost, originality can be stifled, and certain styles of thinking may be discouraged. Furthermore, AI detection tools are notoriously error prone5, watermarks left by AI agents can be erased by rewriting the text6, and humans don’t seem particularly motivated to carefully look over the writing (Figure 2).

Areas of consensus — and dissensus

Like past weeks, major disagreements emerged among seminar participants. In addition to debating the inclusion of a rat genitalia case study, we debated the appropriateness of “fighting AI with AI”. For example, AI is increasingly being used in the peer-review process – often without the consent of the original authors8. For an already-overwhelmed peer-review system, this seems necessary for keeping up, checking tedious details, and addressing their own blind spots as humans. However, storing details of unpublished manuscripts in AI chat logs often violates confidentiality norms9. AI agents can be fooled by messages secretly injected into manuscripts10.1 And at the end of the day, it remains unclear: who is accountable if/when the literature becomes over-endowed with junk?
