Ethics: What Should Count?

NoteThis content was identified, synthesized, and written by humans.

👽 Yet, the idea that super-intelligent beings can fundamentally change the nature of science was well-prophesied in a science fiction story published in Nature in 20001.

In November 2026, Stanford psychologist Michael C. Frank made a remarkable announcement on Bluesky: he had used Claude to audit every journal paper he had co-authored in the past five years, finding evidence of at least one error in more than half of those with shared code and data2. As described by a commentator, one reason this announcement was remarkable was because Frank helped advance the open science reform that makes the detection of such errors possible in the first place3. Another reason the announcement was remarkable was because it included an audit of work co-authored with the course instructor. This audit revealed that three columns of potentially sensitive data had been inadvertently uploaded to the repository, a mistake that was easy to correct but whose lessons feel difficult to ignore. Like Frank, the course instructor had dedicated their career to advocating for improvements in the quality of science — even once placing a $3,000 error detection bounty on their own research4. Yet, errors remain, and were discovered in the middle of Week 12 of the Fall 2026 Collective [Artificial] Intelligence Seminar (Figure 1).

Ironically, this week’s seminar began with a discussion of a paper that is guaranteed to have none of the aforementioned computational errors or data leaks (Figure 2): Michael C. Frank’s proposal that scientists be evaluated by a select number of the best publications5. This emphasis on quality over quantity is particularly intriguing as AI makes it easier than ever to both (a) check the quality of past work, and (b) blitz forward with new work. Regardless of the direction researchers head, one group of seminar participants described the importance curiosity-related values in life and science, citing it as a fundamental driver of human behavior6. AI seems capable of both feeding and squashing human curiosity, an insight that is relevant to our roles both as researchers and educators. Indeed, one top-ranking presentation described the challenges and opportunities AI presents to them in the classroom. In one study, researchers reported that high school students seemingly performed better when practicing mathematics with the aid of AI — only to perform worse when tested without the tool7. This effect was mitigated when AI tools gave guidance instead of answers, highlighting ways in which AI may facilitate vs. hinder learning. Seminar participants thus discussed how their roles as educators may further shift from (a) merely delivering facts, to (b) fostering independent thinking, reflection, debate, and a sense of meaning in ones intellectual pursuits.

Areas of consensus — and dissensus

People do not agree about what should be valued and prioritized in the age of AI. Fortunately, identifying areas of consensus and dissensus in a collaborative setting was one of the primary goals of the course!

There was consensus agreement with Frank’s proposal: evaluate scientists based on the best work, not the sheer number of papers or citations they produce5. There was also consensus that academic researchers need better incentives for building the infrastructure that better enables AI-driven discovery. Indeed, many of the most impressive accomplishments in AI-driven science (e.g., AlphaFold) have occurred in domains with strong data infrastructure and well-coordinated research communities8,9. Some explicitly frame AI for science as a “collective social project”9, requiring community, coordination, cross-disciplinary education, shared benchmarks, and accessible infrastructure. This emphasis on the importance of collaboration felt particularly poignant as we approached the end of our very own collaborative effort to create this guide.

Of course, collaboration often involves navigating disagreements10,11. In a world where researchers increasingly rely on AI to find papers, seminar participants disagreed about the appropriateness of optimizing research outputs for AI discovery, consumption, and use. We also disagreed about the extent to which scientists should be rewarded for pursuing curiosity for the sake of curiosity (as opposed to more papers, grant proposals, or institutional initiatives). This, of course, is a rather fundamental disagreement about how academics should spend their time — disagreements we now understood explicitly as we entered the final week of the Fall 2026 Collective [Artificial] Intelligence Seminar (Epilogue: Thoughts for the Next Gen).
